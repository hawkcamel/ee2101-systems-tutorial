    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    Why does a circuit answer one frequency far more strongly than the rest?

    Resonance: when L and C cancel.

    At one special frequency an inductor and a capacitor cancel each other out. Find that frequency, measure how sharp the peak is, and see why a tiny source voltage can build up a huge voltage inside the circuit.

    Push a child on a swing at just the right rhythm and small pushes build a big swing. Push at the wrong rhythm and nothing much happens. An RLC circuit behaves the same way. A radio uses this to pick one station out of hundreds; a wireless phone charger uses it to send power across a gap. In this lesson you will find the "right rhythm" of a circuit, and measure how picky it is.

    • find the resonant frequency ω₀ of a series or parallel RLC circuit
    • find the half-power frequencies ω₁ and ω₂ and the bandwidth B
    • find the quality factor Q, and explain what a high Q means
    • use the duality between series and parallel resonance

    This lesson follows Sections 14.5 and 14.6 of Sadiku. You met resonance briefly in the AC analysis lesson. Here we measure it properly.

    Step 1Series resonance

    Take R, L and C in series, driven by vs = Vmcos ωt. Add up the impedances:

    Only one part of Z changes with ω. Which part, and at what frequency does it vanish?

    Check your guess

    The imaginary part, the reactance ωL − 1/(ωC). The inductor's part grows with ω and the capacitor's part shrinks. They are equal, and cancel, when ωL = 1/(ωC).

    Set the reactance to zero and solve for ω:

    This is the resonant frequency. Notice what happens there:

    • Z = R. The impedance is as small as it can be, so the current is as big as it can be: I = Vm/R.
    • The current is in phase with the source. The power factor is 1, just as for a resistor.
    • The inductor and capacitor voltages are equal in size and opposite in direction, so they cancel. But each one on its own can be much bigger than the source.

    Below ω₀ the capacitor's reactance wins and the circuit looks capacitive. Above ω₀ the inductor wins and it looks inductive.

    Step 2Bandwidth and the half-power frequencies

    The current is biggest at ω₀ and falls away on either side. How quickly does it fall? We measure the width of the peak at the points where the power has dropped to half its peak value.

    The quality factor Q

    How sharp is the peak compared with where it sits? Divide the resonant frequency by the bandwidth:

    A high Q means a tall, narrow peak: the circuit is very picky about frequency. A low Q means a broad, gentle bump. Q also has an energy meaning: it is 2π times the energy stored in L and C, divided by the energy lost in R in one cycle.

    Step 4Parallel resonance

    Now put R, L and C in parallel, driven by a current source Imcos ωt. For parallel elements, admittances add:

    Compare it with the series Z. It has the same shape, with R ↔ 1/R and L ↔ C swapped. This swap is called duality, and it means every series result has a parallel twin. The imaginary part vanishes at the same frequency:

    At resonance Y = 1/R is as small as it can be, so the voltage V = ImR is as big as it can be. The inductor and capacitor currents cancel, and each is Q times the source current.

    Raising R makes a series circuit less sharp (Q = ω₀L/R falls), but a parallel circuit more sharp (Q = ω₀RC rises). In parallel, a big R means less current leaks away through the resistor.

    Step 5Worked examples

    Worked example 1: series

    R = 2 Ω, L = 1 mH and C = 0.4 µF in series, driven by v = 20 cos ωt V. Find ω₀, Q, B, ω₁ and ω₂, the power at ω₀ and ω₁, and the capacitor voltage at resonance.

    A 20 V source puts 500 V across the capacitor. That is resonance at work.

    Worked example 2: parallel

    R = 8 kΩ, L = 0.2 mH and C = 8 µF in parallel. Find ω₀, Q and B.

    A Q of 1600 makes an extremely narrow peak: it is only 15.6 rad/s wide, at 25 000 rad/s.

    A radio tuner uses a series RLC with L = 10 µH and R = 5 Ω. Which C tunes it to 1 MHz, and what are Q and the bandwidth in hertz?

    Answer

    ω₀ = 2π × 10⁶ rad/s, so C = 1/(ω₀²L) = 1/((6.283 × 10⁶)² × 10⁻⁵) = 2.53 nF. Q = ω₀L/R = 62.8/5 = 12.6. B = R/L = 5 × 10⁵ rad/s, which is 5 × 10⁵/(2π) ≈ 80 kHz.

    You double R in a parallel RLC. What happens to its Q?

    • Resonance happens at ω₀ = 1/√(LC), where the inductor and capacitor reactances cancel. The source then sees only R.
    • The half-power frequencies ω₁ and ω₂ bound the bandwidth B = ω₂ − ω₁, and Q = ω₀/B measures how sharp the peak is.
    • Series: B = R/L, Q = ω₀L/R, and |VL| = |VC| = QVm. Parallel is the dual: B = 1/(RC), Q = ω₀RC, and |IL| = |IC| = QIm.
