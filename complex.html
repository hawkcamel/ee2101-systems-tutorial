    PART 1 · WARM-UP

    Why do engineers draw numbers as arrows?

    Complex numbers are arrows.

    Before we touch a circuit we need one tool: a number that has a size and a direction. It is the tool that makes sine waves easy.

    Almost every AC calculation in this course comes down to multiplying and dividing complex numbers. If you can picture them as arrows, the later lessons feel like geometry. If you only memorise rules, each lesson gets harder than the last. Spend twenty minutes here and the rest of the course gets easier.

    • switch between x + jy and r∠θ without a calculator for easy cases
    • draw s − z₁ as the arrow that starts at z₁ and ends at s
    • multiply two complex numbers by multiplying lengths and adding angles

    Step 1One point, two ways to describe it

    Think of q = x + jy as directions on a map: walk x to the right, then y up. You could give the same spot another way: "face angle θ, then walk r". Both describe the same point. Engineers write the second version as r∠θ ("r at angle θ").

    The length r is called the magnitude. The angle θ is called the phase (or argument), measured anticlockwise from the positive real axis. We write j for √−1 because in circuits i already means current.

    Plain atan(y/x) gives the wrong angle on the left half of the plane. For −1 − j, y/x = 1, so atan says 45°. The arrow actually points down and to the left, at −135°. Use atan2, or your calculator's rectangular-to-polar key, which looks at the signs of x and y separately.

    Drag the dot or type new coordinates. Try a point in each of the four quadrants and check that the angle makes sense. At the origin the length is zero and the angle has no meaning.

    Step 2The arrow from z₁ to s

    Here is the one picture you will use again and again. Put two points on the plane, s and z₁. The difference s − z₁ answers the question "how do I get from z₁ to s?" It is the arrow that starts at z₁ and ends at s.

    Example: s = 1 + 2j and z₁ = −1. Then s − z₁ = 2 + 2j: two steps right, two steps up. Its length r₁ is the distance between the two points.

    Put z₁ at the origin. What will the purple and orange arrows look like? Then drag s on top of z₁. What is the length now?

    Purple arrow: from z₁ to s. Its length is the distance |s − z₁|.

    Orange dashed arrow: the same arrow slid back to start at the origin. It has the same length and the same angle. Sliding an arrow without turning or stretching it doesn't change the complex number.

    The grey dashed lines show the horizontal and vertical steps.

    |s − z₁| is just the distance from z₁ to s. In Lesson 9 every pole and every zero of a circuit becomes one of these arrows, and the circuit's gain becomes "multiply some distances, divide by others".

    Step 3Multiply lengths, add angles

    j is the arrow of length 1 pointing straight up (1∠90°). What do you get if you multiply j by j? Think in arrows, not algebra.

    Check your guess

    Lengths: 1 × 1 = 1. Angles: 90° + 90° = 180°. An arrow of length 1 pointing left is −1. So j² = −1, and you can see why: multiplying by j turns an arrow through a quarter turn. You'll meet this again when inductors and capacitors shift a sine wave by 90°.

    To multiply, multiply the lengths and add the angles. To divide, divide the lengths and subtract the bottom angle from the top one. This is why engineers flip to polar form for multiplication and division. Adding and subtracting are easier in x + jy form.

    1. Choose A × B. Drag s around and watch the output angle: it is always θ_A + θ_B.
    2. Switch to A ÷ B and put p₁ very close to s. The output arrow gets very long. Remember this; it is what a "pole" does.

    • A complex number is an arrow: x + jy says "right and up", r∠θ says "how far, which way".
    • s − z₁ is the arrow from z₁ to s, and its length is the distance between them.
    • Multiply means multiply the lengths and add the angles. Multiplying by j is a quarter turn.
