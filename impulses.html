    Can any signal be built from tiny kicks?

    Building a signal out of kicks.

    Chop a signal into thin slices, squeeze each slice into one sharp kick, and add up what the circuit does with each kick.

    How do you find out what a bell sounds like? You don't play it a song. You strike it once and listen to it ring. That ring tells you how the bell will react to anything. Engineers test circuits, loudspeakers, buildings and concert halls the same way. They hit the thing with one short, sharp kick (an impulse) and record what comes back (the impulse response). This lesson shows why that one recording is enough.

    • approximate a signal by thin rectangles, and explain why each one's area matters
    • describe the impulse δ(t) as "a pulse squeezed thinner and taller, keeping area 1"
    • predict the output of an LTI circuit by adding up shifted, scaled copies of its impulse response

    Step 1Slice the signal into a table

    Suppose you measure a voltage every Δt seconds and write the readings in a table. One simple picture of the signal is to hold each reading flat until the next one. That gives a row of thin rectangles.

    Pick a signal and a spacing. Click a row in the table, or use the slider, to highlight one rectangle. All three signals are zero outside −5 ≤ t ≤ 5.

    Grey: the real signal. Teal: each reading held flat. Purple: the rectangle you picked.

    The highlighted rectangle is f(tₖ) tall and Δt wide, so its area is f(tₖ)Δt. If it sits below the axis, the area counts as negative.

    Step 2Squeeze a slice into a kick

    Take one rectangle and make it thinner, but keep its area the same. Half the width means twice the height. Try it below with a positive slice and then a negative one.

    Keep squeezing until the width is practically zero. What's left is an impulse: infinitely thin, infinitely tall, with a definite area. We write a unit-area impulse at time tₖ as δ(t − tₖ), the Dirac delta. One with area wₖ is wₖδ(t − tₖ). Because it has no sensible height, we draw it as an arrow and write its area next to it.

    Hitting a cricket ball. The bat touches the ball for about a millisecond. The exact force-versus-time curve hardly matters. What matters is the total push, force × time. An impulse is that idea taken to the limit: all of the push delivered in an instant.

    Why do we keep the area and not the height?

    A very short burst of current into a capacitor delivers a definite amount of charge, because charge is the area under the current curve. Two bursts with the same area deliver the same charge, even if one is tall and thin and the other short and wide. The capacitor can't tell them apart. So the area is what matters, and the impulse keeps exactly that. (Strictly, δ is a "distribution": it is defined by what it does inside an integral, not by a value at each instant.)

    Step 3One kick for every row

    Do this for every row of the table. Each time tₖ gets one kick, and its area is the reading times the spacing, f(tₖ)Δt.

    Don't drop the Δt. The arrows are areas, not heights. Halve the spacing and you get twice as many kicks, each half the size. Without Δt, refining the table would double the signal.

    As the spacing shrinks to zero, the sum becomes an integral. It says the signal is a smooth row of kicks, each of size f(τ)dτ:

    This is called the sifting property. The impulse "sifts out" the single value f(t) from everything else.

    Step 4Add up the circuit's response to every kick

    Now the payoff. Say a circuit at rest answers a single unit kick with h(t) = e⁻ᵗu(t). (That is an RC circuit with RC = 1 s, by the way.) Two facts from the last lesson:

    • Time-invariant: a kick later gives the same response, just later: h(t − tₖ).
    • Linear: a kick of size wₖ gives a response wₖ times as big.

    The bell-shaped input is symmetric about t = 0. Will the output be symmetric too? Where do you think its peak will be: before t = 0, at t = 0, or after it?

    Check your guess

    After t = 0, and not symmetric. Each kick's response only rings forwards in time, so the output lags the input and gets a long tail on the right. Use the controls below to watch it build up.

    Add rows one at a time. Each new kick adds one more shifted copy of h(t) to the output. A negative reading subtracts a copy.

    Quick check

    A table reading is f(tₖ) = 2 and the spacing is Δt = 0.1 s. How big is that row's kick?

    • Any signal can be sliced into thin rectangles, and each one squeezed into a kick with the same area.
    • An LTI circuit answers each kick with a shifted, scaled copy of its impulse response h(t).
    • Add all those copies and you have the output. So h(t) on its own describes the circuit completely.
