    PART 4 · THE S-DOMAIN

    What is a signal made of?

    Building a signal from sine waves.

    Part 3 chopped signals into kicks. This lesson splits them into sine waves instead, and then adds one small idea that turns Fourier into Laplace.

    The equaliser in a music app has sliders for bass, mid and treble. It can only work because any sound can be split into sine waves of different frequencies, each turned up or down, and added back together. Fourier showed this in 1807. In Part 2 you learned how a circuit treats one sine wave. Put the two together and you can predict what a circuit does to any signal.

    • build a rough square wave by adding a few sine waves
    • say in words what a Fourier transform measures
    • explain why Laplace multiplies the signal by e−σt first

    Step 1Build a waveform by adding waves

    Start with one 1 Hz sine wave. Then mix in some 3 Hz and 5 Hz. These are called harmonics: whole-number multiples of the lowest, or fundamental, frequency.

    Square waves have flat tops and sharp edges. Sine waves have neither. Do you think you can get anything close to a square wave just by adding sines?

    Find out

    Press "Try a square-wave approximation". Three sines already look surprisingly square. The corners stay rounded and there is a small overshoot at each jump. A sharp edge needs infinitely many harmonics.

    The individual sine waves

    Their sum

    Teal is 1 Hz, orange is 3 Hz, purple is 5 Hz. Now try the 3 Hz phase slider with the square preset loaded. The same three ingredients, shifted a little in time, give a completely different shape. Phase matters.

    This is why sharp edges contain high frequencies. A low-pass filter strips out the high harmonics, so a square wave going through it comes out with rounded corners. You'll see this happen in the playground.

    Step 2From a list of harmonics to a spectrum

    A repeating signal is made of a list of harmonics: the Fourier series. A one-off signal, such as a single pulse, needs a smooth spread of every frequency: the Fourier transform. Both answer the same question: how much of each frequency is in this signal?

    Read e−jωt as a test wave at frequency ω. The integral measures how well the signal matches that test wave. A big result means "lots of this frequency in here". The angle of the result tells you the phase.

    Step 3What Laplace adds: a fade-out dial

    There's a catch. The Fourier integral only works if the signal dies away. A signal that keeps going, like a step, or keeps growing, like eᵗ, makes the integral blow up. Laplace's fix is to multiply the signal by a fading factor e−σt before testing frequencies. Turn σ up far enough and almost anything fades out.

    So s = σ + jω carries two dials. σ is how hard we fade the signal, and ω is which frequency we test. With σ = 0 we're back to plain Fourier, as long as the integral still works there.

    Set a = 0.5, so the signal grows like e0.5t. What is the smallest σ that makes the faded signal die away?

    Check your guess

    Anything bigger than 0.5. The faded signal is e(a−σ)t, which decays only when σ > a. That boundary, σ = a, is the edge of the region of convergence, which you'll meet properly in the next lesson.
