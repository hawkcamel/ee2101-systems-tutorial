    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How much power does an AC circuit really use?

    Power in AC circuits.

    Voltage and current both swing, so the power swings too. Learn which part of it does work, which part just sloshes back and forth, and how engineers keep the bill and the cables under control.

    Your electricity meter counts energy in kilowatt-hours. A factory full of motors can draw far more current than its kilowatts suggest, and the power company charges for that. A phone charger is labelled in watts, but a backup power supply (UPS) is labelled in volt-amperes. By the end of this lesson you will know why these numbers differ, and how one capacitor can fix the factory's problem.

    • find the instantaneous power p(t) and the average power P of an element
    • choose a load that draws the most power from a circuit
    • find the RMS (effective) value of a periodic waveform, and use it in power formulas
    • find the complex power S = P + jQ, the apparent power and the power factor of a load
    • use conservation of complex power, and size a capacitor for power factor correction

    This lesson follows Chapter 11 of Sadiku, Fundamentals of Electric Circuits. Peak phasors (like 10∠30° V) are used for Steps 1 and 2. From Step 3 on we switch to RMS values, which is what engineers use for power.

    Zero, because cos 90° = 0. The power still swings up and down, but the energy that flows in during one quarter cycle flows back out during the next.

    Two special cases are worth remembering:

    • Purely resistive (θᵥ = θᵢ): P = ½VₘIₘ = ½Iₘ²R. A resistor absorbs power all the time.
    • Purely reactive (θᵥ − θᵢ = ±90°): P = 0. An ideal inductor or capacitor absorbs no average power.

    Only the difference θᵥ − θᵢ matters. Slide both angles by the same amount and P does not change.

    Worked example

    v(t) = 120 cos(377t + 45°) V and i(t) = 10 cos(377t − 10°) A. Find the instantaneous and average power absorbed.

    A current i(t) = 5 cos(100t + 20°) A flows through a 10 Ω resistor in series with a 0.1 H inductor. Find the average power.

    Answer

    Only the resistor absorbs average power: P = ½Iₘ²R = ½ × 25 × 10 = 125 W. (Check with the phasors: Z = 10 + j10 = 14.14∠45° Ω, so V = 70.7∠65° V and P = ½ × 70.7 × 5 × cos 45° = 125 W.)

    Step 4Apparent power, power factor and complex power

    Write the average power as two pieces:

    The apparent power S = VrmsIrms is what you would get by multiplying a voltmeter reading by an ammeter reading. The power factor says how much of it is real. The angle θᵥ − θᵢ is called the power factor angle, and it equals the angle of the load impedance Z. A pf of 1 means a resistive load. A pf below 1 is called lagging when the current lags the voltage (an inductive load) and leading when the current leads (a capacitive load).

    The conjugate flips the sign of the current's angle, so the product's angle is θᵥ − θᵢ, exactly the angle we need. The real part is the average power P. The imaginary part is the reactive power Q.

    Q measures the energy that swaps back and forth between the source and the inductors and capacitors each cycle. Q > 0 for an inductive load, Q < 0 for a capacitive one. Using V = ZI, there are two more handy forms. They show that the resistance takes all of P and the reactance takes all of Q:

    Worked example

    A load has v(t) = 60 cos(ωt − 10°) V and i(t) = 1.5 cos(ωt + 50°) A. Find the complex and apparent power, the real and reactive power, the power factor and the load impedance.

    Q is negative and the current leads, so the load is capacitive.

    A load Z = 60 + j40 Ω is connected to 120 Vrms. Find P, Q and the power factor.

    Answer

    |Z| = 72.1 Ω at 33.7°, so Irms = 120/72.1 = 1.664 A. P = I²R = 1.664² × 60 = 166.2 W and Q = I²X = 1.664² × 40 = 110.8 VAR. pf = cos 33.7° = 0.832 lagging.

    Step 5Conservation of AC power

    Energy is conserved, and so is complex power. The complex power delivered by the sources equals the sum of the complex powers absorbed by all the loads, however they are connected:

    Step 6Power factor correction

    Most loads, such as motors, fans and transformers, are inductive, so they have a lagging power factor. For the same real power P, a lower pf means a bigger |S| and therefore a bigger current, since Irms = |S|/Vrms. That extra current heats the cables and transformers but does no useful work. So power companies charge large customers for a poor power factor.

    The fix: put a capacitor in parallel with the load. The load's own voltage and current do not change, so it still gets the same P. But the capacitor's negative Q cancels part of the load's positive Q, and the supply current drops.

    Below, the supply is 230 Vrms at 50 Hz.

    Dashed grey: the load's own S. Orange: the capacitor's QC. Purple: what the supply now provides. P never changes.

    1. Correct to pf 0.95, then to pf 1. Notice how much more capacitance the last bit needs. That is why utilities usually ask for about 0.95, not 1.
    2. Keep adding capacitance past pf 1. The pf falls again, now leading. Over-correcting is as bad as under-correcting.

    Worked example

    A 4 kW load at pf 0.8 lagging is supplied at 120 Vrms, 60 Hz. Find the capacitor that raises the pf to 0.95.

    The supply current falls from 4000/(0.8 × 120) = 41.7 A to 4000/(0.95 × 120) = 35.1 A.

    Step 7Measuring power, and paying for it

    A wattmeter has two coils. The current coil goes in series with the load, like an ammeter. The voltage coil goes across the load, like a voltmeter. The meter's needle responds to the average of their product, so it reads the average power directly:

    A voltmeter and an ammeter alone would only give you the apparent power VrmsIrms.

    The electricity bill

    Your meter adds up energy, which is average power multiplied by time. The unit is the kilowatt-hour: 1 kWh = 1000 W for one hour = 3.6 MJ.

    Worked example

    A 2 kW air-conditioner runs 8 hours a day for a 30-day month, and electricity costs S$0.30 per kWh. What does it cost to run?

    Homes are billed for kWh only. Large industrial customers are also billed for their peak demand (kVA) or for a poor power factor. That is the money that power factor correction saves.
