    PART 4 · THE S-DOMAIN

    What comes out when you switch it on?

    Predicting the output.

    Multiply in the s-domain, come back to time, and see both the messy start and the settled sine wave.

    Switch on a sine-wave source and the output doesn't jump straight to the neat sine wave a Bode plot predicts. First there's a short, untidy start (the transient), and then it settles (the steady state). Sometimes the transient is the bit that matters: a speaker's thump at switch-on, or the voltage spike when a motor starts. One formula gives you both.

    • use Y(s) = H(s)X(s) instead of convolution
    • tell the transient part of an output apart from its steady-state part
    • check a settled sine-wave output against the Bode plot

    The recipe: transform the input, multiply by H(s), then go back to time with partial fractions and the table. We assume the circuit starts at rest (no stored charge).

    4. What the Bode plot predicts

    Why does the Bode plot predict the settled sine wave?

    Feed ejω₀t into a stable LTI circuit and, once it settles, it comes out multiplied by the complex number H(jω₀). Sine and cosine are the imaginary and real parts of ejω₀t, so:

    A sine switched on at t = 0 also produces a transient, which dies away when the poles are on the left. So the steady-state formula tells you where the output ends up, not how it starts.

    Further reading: transfer functions and zero initial conditions · zero-state and zero-input responses.

    • Y(s) = H(s)X(s): one multiplication replaces a convolution.
    • The output = a transient (set by the circuit's poles, it dies away if they're on the left) + a steady state (set by the input).
    • For a sine input, the steady state is |H(jω₀)| times bigger and shifted by ∠H(jω₀). That's the Bode plot again.
