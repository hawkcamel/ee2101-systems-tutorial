    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How can two circuits share energy without touching?

    Coupled coils and transformers.

    Put two coils near each other and a changing current in one makes a voltage in the other. That one idea gives us mutual inductance, the transformer, and the whole electricity grid.

    The power station makes electricity at about 22 kV. The cables across the country run at up to 400 kV. Your phone charges at 5 V. Every one of those steps is done by a transformer: two coils on an iron core, with no wire between them. A wireless phone charger does the same trick through air. In this lesson you will learn how to write circuit equations for coils that share a magnetic field.

    • explain mutual inductance M, and write the voltage it induces
    • use the dot convention to get the sign of the mutual term
    • find the coupling coefficient k and the energy in coupled coils
    • analyse a linear transformer using reflected impedance
    • use the ideal-transformer rules for voltage, current and impedance, and design for maximum power transfer

    This lesson follows Chapter 13 of Sadiku. All sources are sinusoidal, so we use phasors throughout.

    Check your guess

    Yes. The second coil only cares about the changing flux through it, not where the flux came from. So a changing i₁ induces a voltage in coil 2.

    Step 2The dot convention

    Manufacturers mark one end of each coil with a dot. The dots tell you the sign of the mutual term without you having to look at the windings. The rule is:

    If both currents enter the dotted ends (or both leave them), the mutual term is +jωM. If one current enters a dotted end and the other enters an undotted end, the mutual term is −jωM.

    Below, both currents are drawn entering the top ends. Move the dot on coil 2 and watch the sign change.

    Coils in series

    Join two coupled coils in series. One current i flows through both. If it enters both dotted ends, the coils help each other (series-aiding). If it enters one dot and leaves the other, they fight (series-opposing):

    L₁ = 2 mH, L₂ = 8 mH and M = 3 mH. Find the total inductance for series-aiding and series-opposing.

    Answer

    Aiding: 2 + 8 + 6 = 16 mH. Opposing: 2 + 8 − 6 = 4 mH. Measuring both and subtracting is a practical way to find M: (16 − 4)/4 = 3 mH.

    Step 5The ideal transformer

    Now make the coupling perfect (k = 1), make both inductances very large, and remove all losses. That is an ideal transformer. All the flux links both coils, so each turn of wire sees the same voltage. A coil with more turns gets proportionally more voltage. With N₁ turns on the primary and N₂ on the secondary:

    n is the turns ratio. If n > 1 it is a step-up transformer, and if n < 1 a step-down. Notice that the voltage goes up by n while the current goes down by n. Multiply them and n cancels:

    A transformer can't create power. It trades voltage for current. Signs follow the dots: if V₁ and V₂ are both positive at the dotted ends, take +n. If I₁ enters a dot and I₂ leaves one, take +1/n. Otherwise flip the sign.

    Step 6Applications

    Impedance matching

    In the power lesson, a source with resistance Rs gave its most power to a load equal to Rs. But what if the load is fixed at a different value, like an 8 Ω speaker on an amplifier with an 800 Ω output? Put a transformer in between and choose n so that the load looks like Rs:

    For the speaker, n = √(8/800) = 0.1: a 10:1 step-down. Try it below with a 100 V source that has Rs = 50 Ω.

    Sending power far away

    Power lost in a cable is I²R. For a fixed power P = VI, a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the loss falls with the square of the current.

    Worked example

    Send 100 kW (at unity power factor) through a cable with total resistance 0.5 Ω. Compare 400 V with 22 kV.

    At 400 V almost a third of the power is wasted. At 22 kV the loss is a tiny 0.01%. That is why the grid steps the voltage up to transmit it, then back down near your home. Only AC can do this so simply, because a transformer needs a changing current.

    Isolation

    The two sides of a transformer are joined only by magnetic flux, not by wire. That isolates them: a fault on one side can't put a dangerous DC voltage on the other. That is why bathroom shaver sockets and medical equipment use isolation transformers.

    • A changing current in one coil induces v = M di/dt in a nearby coil. The dots give the sign: +M when both currents enter dotted ends, −M otherwise. The coupling coefficient is k = M/√(L₁L₂) ≤ 1.
    • A linear transformer reflects its load into the primary: Zin = jωL₁ + ω²M²/(jωL₂ + ZL).
    • An ideal transformer has V₂/V₁ = n, I₂/I₁ = 1/n, power in = power out and Zin = ZL/n². It is used for matching, for transmission at high voltage, and for isolation.
