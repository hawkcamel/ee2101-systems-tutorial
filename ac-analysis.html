Sinusoidal steady-state analysis

Companion exercises for Chapters 9 and 10 of Fundamentals of Electric Circuits. The explanations, values and interactive examples here are original. Use the textbook for the full chapter treatment and problems.

1. Solve at one frequency

After the starting transient has died away, a stable linear circuit driven by a sinusoid responds at the same frequency. We will calculate its amplitude and phase. This calculation does not include the starting transient.

  1. Express the source as a cosine and write its phasor.
  2. Evaluate each impedance at the source frequency.
  3. Use Kirchhoff’s laws to find the required voltage or current.
  4. Restore the cosine time dependence.

In the series circuit below, one current flows through R, L and C. Take the source phase as zero. The voltage across each element is measured from left to right along the top wire.

2. Mesh analysis: follow the loop

The loop equation is Vₛ = (Zᵣ + Zₗ + Z꜀)I. Divide by the total impedance to find the current. Then multiply by the chosen element’s impedance to find its voltage.

Left: add Vᵣ (teal), Vₗ (orange) and V꜀ (purple) head to tail. Their sum is Vₛ (dashed grey). Right: teal is the source and purple is the selected output, both in volts. The horizontal axis spans two source periods.

3. Nodal analysis: account for each branch current

For a second example, connect a source to an output node through R₁. From that node connect R₂ and C to ground. The current entering through R₁ equals the two currents leaving the node.

Vₛ ── R₁ ──┬── Vₒ
           ├── R₂ ── ground
           └── C  ── ground

With R₁ = R₂ = 1 kΩ, C = 1 µF and ω = 1000 rad/s, the ratio is 0.4 − j0.2 = 0.4472∠−26.565°. A 10 cos(1000t) V source therefore gives 4.472 cos(1000t − 26.565°) V at the output.

4. Connect the calculation to frequency response

The calculations above use one frequency. Repeat them at many frequencies and plot the voltage ratio: that gives a frequency response. This extends the Chapter 9–10 methods to the subject of Chapter 14.

The purple marker is the frequency selected above. Each graph covers 1 Hz to 10 kHz on a logarithmic axis. Change R, L or C, then compare the waveform and frequency-response plots.

What resonance means in this circuit

At ω₀ = 1/√(LC), the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel. The total impedance is R, so the current is largest for a fixed source amplitude. This is series resonance. The resistor voltage equals the source voltage and is in phase with it; the inductor and capacitor voltages are equal in magnitude and opposite in phase. Either can exceed the source voltage.

Select the resistor output and reduce R. The gain peak becomes narrower. Select the capacitor output and compare its peak with ω₀: a voltage-ratio peak need not occur at the series-resonance frequency.

5. Other circuit methods

Superposition: keep different frequencies separate

With several sources at the same frequency, add their phasor responses. With different frequencies, solve once for each frequency: the impedances change each time. Convert each answer back to time and add the time signals. To suppress an independent voltage source, short it; to suppress an independent current source, open it. Leave dependent sources active.

Source transformation and Thévenin–Norton equivalents

At a fixed frequency, a voltage source Vₛ in series with Z is equivalent at its terminals to a current source Vₛ/Z in parallel with Z. Match the current-arrow direction to the voltage-source polarity. The open-circuit terminal voltage is Vₜₕ. The short-circuit terminal current is Iₙ. With consistent reference directions, Vₜₕ = ZₜₕIₙ and Zₙ = Zₜₕ.

Find Zₜₕ by suppressing independent sources and looking into the terminals. If dependent sources remain, apply a test source and calculate Vtest/Itest. For a load Zload, the load voltage is Vₜₕ Zload/(Zₜₕ + Zload).

Ideal op-amp circuits in AC

For an ideal inverting amplifier with input impedance Zᵢ and feedback impedance Zf, negative feedback holds the inverting node at the grounded non-inverting node’s voltage. The input current flows through the feedback impedance, giving Vₒ/Vᵢ = −Zf/Zᵢ. This assumes linear operation with effective negative feedback. A real op-amp’s bandwidth and supply limits restrict that approximation.
