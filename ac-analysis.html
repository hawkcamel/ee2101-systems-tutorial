    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How do we solve a circuit driven by a sine wave?

    Solving AC circuits.

    Same KVL and KCL as DC, a four-step recipe, and your first look at resonance.

    An old-style radio picks one station out of dozens using a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor, much like the circuit below. Turning the tuning knob changes C. That moves the one frequency the circuit lets through most easily. By the end of this page you'll be able to find that frequency yourself.

    • follow the four-step phasor recipe for any single-frequency circuit
    • use mesh and nodal analysis with impedances
    • find the resonant frequency of a series RLC circuit and explain what happens there
    • use superposition (including sources at different frequencies), Thévenin, Norton and op-amp circuits with impedances
    • simplify networks with delta–wye conversion, and explain how phase shifters and AC bridges work

    Step 1The recipe

    Once the switch-on wobble has died away, a circuit driven by a sine wave settles into a sine wave at the same frequency. Only its size and timing change. (We'll study the wobble, the "transient", in Part 3.) To find the settled wave:

    1. Phasor in. Write the source as a cosine and turn it into a phasor.
    2. Impedances. Replace every R, L and C by its impedance at the source frequency.
    3. Solve. Use KVL, KCL, voltage dividers, whatever you'd use in a DC circuit.
    4. Phasor out. Turn the answer back into a cosine.

    In the series circuit below, one current I flows through R, L and C. The source has phase zero.

    At a very low frequency, which element takes almost all of the source voltage? (Hint: what does a capacitor look like at DC?)

    Check your guess

    The capacitor. At low frequency its impedance 1/(ωC) is huge, so almost all of the voltage appears across it, and very little current flows. Set the output to "Across C", drag the frequency down, and check.

    Step 2Mesh analysis: walk round the loop

    Walk round the loop once and add up the voltage drops: Vₛ = (Z_R + Z_L + Z_C)I. Divide to get the current. Multiply the current by one element's impedance to get the voltage across that element.

    Look at the bracket ωL − 1/(ωC). The inductor and capacitor pull in opposite directions. At one special frequency they cancel exactly, and the circuit looks like a plain resistor. That is resonance.

    1. Slide the frequency slowly through resonance. Watch the orange and purple arrows: below resonance V_C is longer, above it V_L is longer.
    2. At resonance the orange and purple arrows cancel. Notice that each one can be longer than the source arrow.

    Step 4From one frequency to all of them

    So far we solved at one frequency. Now repeat the same calculation at every frequency and plot the answer. That plot is the circuit's frequency response, and it is the main subject of this whole course.

    Decibels (dB) squash a huge range of ratios onto a readable scale. 0 dB means the output is as big as the input. −20 dB means one tenth. −40 dB means one hundredth.

    Click or drag on either graph to pick a frequency. These controls stay in sync with the ones at the top of the page.

    What resonance means here

    At ω₀ = 1/√(LC) the inductor and capacitor cancel, the total impedance is just R, and the current is as large as it can be. The resistor voltage then equals the source voltage. The inductor and capacitor voltages are equal in size and opposite in direction, and each can be bigger than the source.

    1. Choose "Across R" and lower R. The peak gets narrower and sharper. That is how a radio picks out one station and rejects its neighbours.
    2. Choose "Across C". Is its peak exactly at ω₀? (Look closely. It isn't quite.)
    3. Compare the three outputs: across R passes a band of frequencies, across C passes low ones, across L passes high ones. One circuit gives three filters.

    Step 5More tools from DC that still work

    Every DC theorem you know carries over to phasors, because impedances obey the same algebra as resistances. Here are the four you will use most, each with a worked example.

    Superposition, and sources at different frequencies

    Several sources at the same frequency: solve with one source at a time and add the phasor answers. Sources at different frequencies: you must use superposition. Solve once for each frequency (the impedances change each time), turn each answer back into a cosine, and add the time signals. Never add phasors that belong to different frequencies.

    To switch a source off, replace a voltage source with a wire and a current source with a gap. Leave dependent sources on.

    Source transformation, Thévenin and Norton

    At a fixed frequency, a voltage source Vs in series with Z behaves exactly like a current source Vs/Z in parallel with the same Z. Any two-terminal circuit reduces to a Thévenin equivalent (VTh in series with ZTh) or a Norton equivalent (IN in parallel with ZN = ZTh):

    • VTh is the open-circuit voltage at the terminals.
    • IN is the short-circuit current, and VTh = ZThIN.
    • ZTh: switch off the independent sources and find the impedance seen from the terminals. With dependent sources present, apply a test source and compute Vtest/Itest.

    Worked example: a Thévenin equivalent

    A source 10∠0° V in series with 4 Ω feeds terminals a–b. Across a–b there is a capacitor with ZC = −j4 Ω. Find the Thévenin and Norton equivalents seen from a–b.

    Check: shorting a–b also shorts the capacitor, so the short-circuit current is 10/4 = 2.5 A. It matches. The next lesson shows that a load of 2 + j2 Ω, the conjugate of ZTh, takes the most power from this circuit.

    Zi = 10 kΩ and Zf is 10 kΩ in parallel with 10 nF. Find the gain at ω = 10⁴ rad/s.

    Answer

    ωRC = 10⁴ × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸ = 1, so Zf = R/(1 + jωRC) = 10 000/(1 + j) Ω. The gain is −Zf/Zi = −1/(1 + j) = 0.707∠135°. That is −3 dB, with the output shifted by 135°.

    Step 6Delta–wye: when nothing is in series or parallel

    Look at the network on the left below. Try to find the impedance between two of its corners. Are any two impedances in series? In parallel? No. Every one of them touches a node that has three branches. Series and parallel rules can't start.

    The trick is to swap one shape for another that behaves exactly the same from the outside. Three impedances joined in a triangle are called a delta (Δ). Three impedances meeting at one centre point are called a wye (Y). For the right values, a Δ and a Y are indistinguishable at their three terminals a, b and c.

    Δ to Y

    In words: each Y arm equals the product of the two Δ sides that touch its node, divided by the sum of all three Δ sides.

    Y to Δ

    In words: each Δ side equals the sum of the products of the Y arms taken two at a time, divided by the Y arm on the opposite side.

    These are the same formulas you met for resistors. The only change is that the numbers are complex. When all three are equal, a balanced network, the formulas collapse to something easy to remember:

    Worked example

    A Δ has Za = j4 Ω, Zb = 2 Ω and Zc = −j4 Ω. Find the equivalent Y.

    Check by going back: Z₁Z₂ + Z₂Z₃ + Z₃Z₁ = −j32 + j32 + 16 = 16, so Za = 16/(−j4) = j4 Ω. It matches.

    A balanced Δ has 12 + j9 Ω in each side. What is the equivalent Y? (You will use exactly this in the three-phase lesson.)

    Answer

    ZY = ZΔ/3 = 4 + j3 Ω in each arm.

    Step 7Application: phase shifters

    Sometimes you need a copy of a signal that is shifted in time: say, 30° ahead of the original. Phasors tell us an RC circuit can do it. Feed the source into R and C in series and take the output across one of them.

    The output across R and the output across C: which one leads the source, and which one lags?

    Check your guess

    The current through a capacitor leads its voltage. So the series current leads the source, and the resistor voltage, which follows the current, leads. The capacitor voltage lags the current by 90°, so it lags the source.

    Look at the phasor picture. VR and VC are always at right angles, and they always add up to Vs. So the tip of VR must sit on a semicircle with Vs as its diameter. As ωRC changes, the tip slides round the semicircle and the phase shift changes.

    A single stage never reaches 90°: to get there, the output would shrink to zero. To shift further, connect stages one after another (cascade them). Each stage loads the one before it, so you can't simply add the angles. You have to solve the whole circuit.

    Worked example: a 90° leading shift

    Two leading stages (series C, then R to ground, twice) with R = 20 Ω and 1/(ωC) = 20 Ω. Find Vo/Vi.

    The output leads by exactly 90°, but it is only a third as big. Phase shift costs amplitude.
