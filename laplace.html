    PART 4 · THE S-DOMAIN

    How does Laplace turn calculus into algebra?

    The Laplace workshop.

    Learn a few transform pairs, see how moving a pole changes the signal, then use it all to solve an RC circuit in three lines.

    Solving an RLC circuit directly means solving a differential equation, every time. The Laplace transform swaps each d/dt for a multiplication by s, so the same problem becomes algebra you could do in secondary school. The price is that you have to translate into the s-domain and back out again. This lesson teaches that translation. Most of it is just reading a table.

    • compute a simple transform straight from the definition, and say where its region of convergence comes from
    • link a pole's position to the shape of the time signal: decaying, growing or oscillating
    • go back to time with partial fractions and a table
    • solve the RC step response using Laplace

    Step 3Move a pole, watch the signal change

    A transform pair is the same signal written two ways. For an exponential the link is very direct:

    The pole (where the bottom of the fraction is zero) is at s = a. Pole on the left (a < 0): the signal dies away. Pole on the right (a > 0): it grows. For oscillating signals the poles come in pairs. The real part sets how fast the swing dies away, and the imaginary part sets how fast it oscillates.

    Left = decays, right = grows, up/down = wiggles. Where a pole sits on the s-plane tells you what the signal does in time. Engineers read pole plots like this all the time.

    Time domain: change the waveform

    The "envelope" is the pair of curves that a wiggling signal stays between. A ratio of 0.5 means the swing halves every second. A ratio of 1 means it never dies down.

    s-domain: drag the poles

    Crosses: poles. Circle: a zero, if there is one. Green: the region of convergence (its edge is not included).

    1. Exponential: move the pole from −1 to −2. Does the signal die faster or slower? Now drag it to +0.5.
    2. Sine: keep the real part fixed and double the imaginary part. The period halves and the envelope stays the same. Then change the period on the left and watch the poles move.
    3. Step, ramp and delayed step all have a pole at s = 0. What's different about their transforms?

    PayoffSolve the RC circuit in three lines

    Remember the RC circuit from the “What is a system?” lesson? Its equation was RC y′ + y = x. Starting at rest, transform every term using "derivative → × s":

    No calculus left. For a step input, X(s) = 1/s. Split it into partial fractions and read the answer off the table:

    That is the same curve you watched climb in the “What is a system?” lesson, and the same one convolution gave in the convolution lesson. Three methods, one answer.

    The fraction H(s) = 1/(1 + sRC) is the circuit's transfer function. It is the Laplace transform of the impulse response h(t). Put s = jω and you get exactly the 1/(1 + jωRC) from the phasor lesson. One function connects Parts 2, 3 and 4.

    Quick check

    What do you get from the inverse Laplace transform of H(s)?

    • X(s) = ∫x(t)e−stdt, and it only exists inside its region of convergence.
    • Pole position tells you the signal's shape: left decays, right grows, off the real axis wiggles.
    • d/dt → ×s turns circuit equations into algebra. The result Y = H(s)X introduces the transfer function H(s).
