LESSON 03 / FREQUENCY RESPONSE

Frequency response.

Use the geometry of complex factors to read gain and phase.

From roots to response

In Lesson 1, we factored H(s). In Lesson 2, we learned how complex products and quotients work. A pole–zero plot simply marks the zeros zₖ and poles pₖ in the s-plane.

Use the polar form from Lesson 2 for every factor. Write r for a distance to a zero and d for a distance to a pole:

These polar expressions apply where the factors and K are nonzero. At a zero of H, its magnitude is zero and its phase is undefined.

The magnitude is |K| times the product of distances from the purple point s to the zeros, divided by the product of distances to the poles. The full complex H(s) also includes phase: add the angles of the zero vectors, subtract the angles of the pole vectors, and include the phase of K.

A Bode diagram shows both magnitude and phase as functions of frequency. Set s = jω and walk along the imaginary axis. Standard Bode plots use ω > 0 on a logarithmic axis.

How do we find a factor’s magnitude?

Choose a root to highlight its vector. Angles are measured from the positive real direction at the root to the arrow pointing toward s.

Here a is the root you selected, with real part aᵣ and imaginary part aᵢ. Use Pythagoras to find the length:

The horizontal and vertical differences are the legs of a right triangle. Its hypotenuse is the factor’s magnitude.

The distance ledger

An empty product equals 1. Numerator distances increase gain; denominator distances reduce it. Angles add in the numerator and subtract in the denominator.

FROM A MOVING DOT TO A CURVE

A Bode plot is a journey up the imaginary axis.

Set σ = 0, then move s = jω. Each stop produces a magnitude and a phase. Plot those measurements against a logarithmic frequency axis: that is the Bode diagram.

A full imaginary-axis traversal runs from −j∞ to +j∞. A standard Bode plot uses ω > 0: zero and negative frequencies cannot be placed directly on a logarithmic axis. For real-coefficient systems, the negative-frequency magnitude is identical and its phase has the opposite sign (modulo 360°).

What do gain and phase tell us?

The curves above describe the system, not a particular input signal. The Laplace variable is complex; the frequency response evaluates the transfer function on the imaginary axis.

Feed a sine wave into a stable LTI system. After the initial changes have died away, the output is a sine wave at the same frequency. Its amplitude is multiplied by |H(jω)| and its phase is shifted by the angle of H(jω). The initial changes are called the transient response. The input/output lesson shows both the transient and the lasting sine wave.

Recognise three response shapes

Imagine applying sine waves of equal amplitude, one frequency at a time, and waiting for the output to settle. The magnitude plot tells you which frequencies produce a larger or smaller output.

Resonance: a peak

A resonance peak means the system responds more strongly near one frequency than at nearby frequencies. On the magnitude plot, look for a hump. In our two-pole example, moving the complex poles closer to the imaginary axis makes this peak sharper and higher while they stay on the left.

A useful analogy is a swing: repeated pushes near its natural rhythm produce a larger motion.

Notch: a dip

A notch means the system strongly reduces a narrow range of frequencies. A zero exactly at jω₀ makes H(jω₀) = 0, provided no pole is there too. A sine at that frequency has no lasting output after the transient dies away.

All-pass: constant magnitude

An all-pass system has the same gain at every frequency, but its phase can change. In this preset the gain is 1, or 0 dB: sine-wave amplitudes stay the same, while their phase shifts depend on frequency.

PREDICT → MOVE → EXPLAIN

Three small experiments. Three big ideas.

EXPERIMENT 01

Can you make a resonance?

Load Resonance. Set ω = 2. Move p₁ closer to the imaginary axis while keeping it on the left. What happens to its vector length and the peak?

Reveal the reasoning

The pole vector becomes shorter. It divides the numerator, so a smaller denominator generally raises the magnitude. The conjugate pole and any zeros also contribute. If the pole reaches the imaginary axis with no cancelling zero, H is undefined at that frequency; the settled-sine interpretation no longer applies.

EXPERIMENT 02

Can you silence one frequency?

Load A notch. Move the probe to ω = 2. Which distance becomes zero? Now move the zero pair off the imaginary axis.

Reveal the reasoning

At s = z₁, one numerator factor is zero. The magnitude becomes zero (−∞ dB), and phase is undefined. Moving the zero off the axis turns an exact notch into a finite dip.

EXPERIMENT 03

Can phase change without gain?

Load Same gain, new phase. Compare the distances to the mirrored roots on either side of the imaginary axis.

Reveal the reasoning

Mirrored zeros and poles are equally distant from every point on the imaginary axis. Their distance ratios are 1, so the magnitude stays at 0 dB. Their vector angles differ: phase changes with frequency.

Design shortcuts: why 20 dB per decade?

A Bode magnitude plot is the gain on the imaginary axis, expressed in decibels. The second Bode plot records phase. Use base-10 logarithms and set s = jω, rather than an arbitrary complex s.

A decade means a factor of ten in frequency: 100 Hz to 1 kHz, for example. Multiplying the amplitude ratio by ten adds 20 dB; dividing it by ten subtracts 20 dB. This uses an amplitude ratio such as voltage gain. Power ratios use 10 log₁₀ instead.

It changes smoothly from 0 to −20, passing through −10 dB/decade at the corner. Load RC low-pass and compare 100 kHz with 1 MHz, both well above its 1.592 kHz corner.

One zero: the opposite contribution

The same calculation gives +20 dB/decade well above the zero’s corner. A zero at the origin is different: the factor s contributes +20 dB/decade at every positive frequency. A factor 1/s contributes −20 dB/decade everywhere.

Add contributions to sketch a design

Logarithms turn multiplication into addition. For normalised first-order factors:

  • RC high-pass: the zero at the origin gives +20 dB/decade below the pole corner. Above the corner, the pole adds −20 and the net slope approaches zero.
  • Two poles: the high-frequency slope approaches −40 dB/decade. A complex pole pair may also produce a resonance peak near its natural frequency.
  • One nonzero zero, then one pole: the slope rises toward +20 between sufficiently separated corners, then returns toward zero. Try the one-zero/one-pole preset.

Repeated roots count repeatedly. Exact pole–zero cancellations remove both contributions. Closely spaced corners, resonance and notches need the exact plot; a straight-line sketch can miss them. Magnitude alone also cannot tell left-half-plane roots from their right-half-plane mirrors: phase and stability still matter.
