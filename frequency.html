    PART 4 · THE S-DOMAIN

    How do poles and zeros shape a Bode plot?

    Frequency response.

    Walk a point up the jω axis, measure its distances to the poles and zeros, and you've drawn a Bode plot.

    A speaker's crossover sends the bass to the big woofer and the treble to the small tweeter. Engineers design that filter with Bode plots, often sketched by hand with a ruler, before they build anything. By the end of this page you'll know how to make that sketch, and why the slopes come in steps of 20 dB per decade.

    • work out |H(jω)| from distances on the pole-zero map
    • predict a resonance peak, a notch, or a flat "all-pass" from where the roots sit
    • sketch a Bode magnitude plot using ±20 dB/decade straight lines

    Step 1Gain is a ratio of distances

    In the last lesson you factored H(s). In Lesson 1 you learned that each factor s − z is the arrow from z to s, and that multiplying multiplies the lengths. Put the two together:

    The phase works the same way: add the angles of the zero arrows and subtract the angles of the pole arrows.

    The frequency response is H(s) with s = jω. Move the purple point up the imaginary axis, measure as you go, and plot the results. That plot is the Bode plot.

    Close to a pole, the distance in the bottom gets small, so the gain goes up. Close to a zero, the distance on top gets small, so the gain goes down. That one sentence explains every shape on this page.

    The full polar form, if you want it

    Exactly at a zero the gain is zero and the phase is undefined.

    Where does each distance come from?

    Here a is the root you picked, with real part aᵣ and imaginary part aᵢ:

    The two gaps are the short sides of a right triangle. The arrow is the long side.

    The distance ledger

    With no zeros, the top product is just 1. Distances to zeros multiply the gain, and distances to poles divide it. Angles add for zeros and subtract for poles.

    FROM A MOVING DOT TO A CURVE

    A Bode plot is a walk up the imaginary axis.

    Keep σ = 0 and move s = jω upwards. Every stop gives one magnitude and one phase. Plot them against frequency on a log axis and you have the Bode plot.

    Bode plots only show ω > 0, because zero and negative numbers can't go on a log axis. For real circuits, negative frequencies give the same magnitude with the phase flipped, so nothing is lost.

    What do the gain and phase mean physically?

    Feed a sine wave into a stable circuit and wait for it to settle. The output is a sine wave at the same frequency, |H(jω)| times bigger and shifted by the angle of H(jω). This is exactly what you calculated with phasors in Part 2, now for every frequency at once.

    The next lesson adds the bit before it settles.

    Step 2Three shapes to recognise

    Imagine feeding in sine waves of equal size one frequency at a time, waiting each time for things to settle. The magnitude plot shows which ones come out bigger and which come out smaller.

    Resonance: a peak

    The circuit responds much more strongly near one frequency. This happens when a pair of complex poles sits close to the jω axis. The closer they get, the taller and sharper the peak.

    Like pushing a child on a swing: push in time with its natural rhythm and it goes much higher.

    Notch: a dip

    A zero sitting right on the jω axis makes H(jω₀) = 0. A sine wave at that frequency is wiped out once things settle. Notch filters are used to remove 50 Hz mains hum from audio and ECG recordings.

    All-pass: flat

    Every frequency comes out the same size (0 dB here), but the phase changes with frequency. This works by mirroring each pole with a zero on the other side of the axis, so every distance ratio is 1.

    Use the experiments below to connect each shape to where the roots are. Further reading: Swarthmore's second-order systems tutorial.

    PREDICT → MOVE → EXPLAIN

    Three small experiments.

    Write down your prediction before you press the button. Getting it wrong is how you learn.

    EXPERIMENT 01

    Can you make a resonance?

    Load Resonance. Set ω = 2. Move p₁ closer to the imaginary axis, keeping it on the left. What happens to its arrow length, and to the peak?

    Reveal the reasoning

    The pole arrow gets shorter. It sits on the bottom of the fraction, so a shorter arrow means a bigger gain. If the pole actually reaches the axis, H blows up at that frequency and the circuit never settles. It just oscillates. That's how an oscillator works.

    EXPERIMENT 02

    Can you silence one frequency?

    Load A notch. Move the probe to ω = 2. Which distance becomes zero? Now drag the zero pair a little off the axis.

    Reveal the reasoning

    At s = z₁ a top factor is zero, so the gain is zero (−∞ dB). Move the zero off the axis and the distance never quite reaches zero, so the perfect notch becomes a finite dip.

    EXPERIMENT 03

    Can the phase change while the gain doesn't?

    Load Same gain, new phase. Compare the distances to the mirrored roots on either side of the axis.

    Reveal the reasoning

    Mirror-image roots are the same distance from every point on the jω axis, so each ratio is 1 and the gain stays at 0 dB. Their angles are different, though, so the phase still changes.

    Step 3The ruler trick: why 20 dB per decade?

    A decade is a factor of ten in frequency, for example 100 Hz to 1 kHz. In decibels, ×10 in amplitude is +20 dB and ÷10 is −20 dB.

    One pole, worked out

    Take the RC low-pass again, with ω_p = 1/(RC):

    • Well below ω_p: the squared term is tiny, so M ≈ 0 dB. Flat.
    • Well above ω_p: the squared term dominates, so M ≈ −20 log₁₀(ω/ω_p). Ten times the frequency gives −20 dB. A straight line sloping down at −20 dB/decade.
    • Exactly at ω_p: M = −10 log₁₀2 = −3.01 dB. That's why the cut-off is called the "−3 dB point".

    Sketch with two straight lines: flat at 0 dB up to the corner, then down at −20 dB per decade. The real curve sits 3 dB below the corner of your sketch. Load "RC low-pass" above and compare 100 kHz with 1 MHz, both far above its 1.59 kHz corner. Do they differ by 20 dB?

    The exact slope, if you're curious

    It slides smoothly from 0 to −20, passing −10 dB/decade exactly at the corner.

    A zero does the opposite

    So it gives +20 dB/decade above its corner. A zero right at the origin (a plain factor s) gives +20 dB/decade at every frequency. 1/s gives −20 everywhere.

    Stack them up

    Logs turn multiplication into addition, so the Bode plot of a product is the sum of the pieces:

    • RC high-pass: the zero at the origin rises at +20. After the pole's corner the pole adds −20, so the line goes flat.
    • Two poles: −40 dB/decade at high frequency. A complex pair may also add a resonance bump near the corner.
    • A zero, then a pole: rises at +20 between the two corners, then levels off. Try "One zero + one pole".

    Straight lines are a sketch, not the answer. Corners that are close together, resonance peaks and notches all need the exact curve. Also, magnitude alone can't tell a left-half-plane root from its mirror on the right. Check the phase.

    Quick check

    Far above both corners, what is the slope of a circuit with two poles and no zeros?

    • |H(jω)| = (distances to zeros) / (distances to poles). Near a pole the gain goes up, near a zero it goes down.
    • Poles close to the jω axis give peaks, zeros on it give notches, and mirrored pairs give all-pass.
    • Each pole bends the line by −20 dB/decade, each zero by +20, and the exact curve is 3 dB off at a single corner.
