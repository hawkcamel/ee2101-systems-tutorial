    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How does the grid deliver power so smoothly?

    Three-phase circuits.

    Almost all electricity is generated and sent out as three sine waves, a third of a cycle apart. See why that is so useful, and learn to analyse it by solving just one phase.

    Your home gets 230 V from one of three "phases". The street cable carries all three, and between any two of them there is 400 V. A factory motor connects to all three at once. Power stations generate three-phase because it delivers a constant flow of power, not a pulsing one, and because it needs less copper for the same power. Both facts come out of the maths in this lesson.

    • write the three phase voltages of a balanced source, and explain why they add to zero
    • find line voltages from phase voltages: VL = √3 Vp
    • analyse balanced Y and Δ loads with a single-phase equivalent
    • find the total power of a balanced system, and explain why it is constant
    • explain what goes wrong when the system is unbalanced

    This lesson follows Chapter 12 of Sadiku. Voltages and currents here are RMS values unless a plot says otherwise.

    Step 1Three voltages, a third of a cycle apart

    A three-phase generator has three coils fixed around a spinning magnet, 120° apart. As the magnet turns, each coil makes a sine wave. Because the coils are spaced evenly, the three waves are identical except that each one is a third of a cycle (120°) behind the one before.

    Add the three voltages together at any instant. What do you get?

    Step 2Phase voltages and line voltages

    In a wye (Y) connected source, the three coils share one common point, the neutral n. That gives four wires: lines a, b and c, plus the neutral. There are two kinds of voltage you can measure:

    • a phase voltage, from a line to neutral, like Van;
    • a line voltage, from one line to another, like Vab.

    The line voltage is just a KVL subtraction:

    Notice two things. The line voltage is √3 times bigger than the phase voltage, and it is 30° ahead. On the phasor diagram, the three line voltages form a triangle joining the tips of the phase voltages.

    Singapore's supply: Vp = 230 V, so VL = √3 × 230 ≈ 400 V. That is where "230/400 V" on equipment labels comes from.

    Step 4Delta-connected loads

    A load can also be connected in delta (Δ): each impedance ZΔ goes straight across two lines, so it sees a line voltage. Its current is a phase current, like IAB = VAB/ZΔ. Each line carries the difference of two phase currents (KCL at a corner):

    Step 6Unbalanced systems

    Real loads are not always equal. A block of flats might have more appliances switched on in one phase than another. Go back to Step 3 and move the "phase c only" slider. Notice:

    • The three currents are no longer equal and 120° apart, so they no longer cancel.
    • The difference flows back through the neutral: IN = Ia + Ib + Ic ≠ 0. That is why houses need a neutral wire.
    • The single-phase shortcut no longer works. Solve each phase separately (with a neutral), or use nodal or mesh analysis on the whole circuit.

    If the neutral is missing in an unbalanced Y–Y system, the load's centre point drifts away from 0 V. Some phases then get more than 230 V and others less. Don't assume the phase voltages stay at 230 V unless the system is balanced or the neutral is connected.

    • For a balanced load, solve phase a as a single-phase circuit (turn a Δ into a Y with ZY = ZΔ/3), then shift by ±120°. The neutral current is zero.
    • The total power P = √3 VLILcos θ is constant in time. If the load is unbalanced, the neutral carries current and each phase must be solved on its own.
