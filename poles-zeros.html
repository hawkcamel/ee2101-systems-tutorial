    PART 4 · THE S-DOMAIN

    Which numbers tell you what a circuit will do?

    Poles and zeros.

    Write a circuit's transfer function, factor the top and the bottom, and a handful of numbers describe the whole circuit.

    Give an experienced engineer a pole-zero plot, just a few crosses and circles on a graph, and within seconds they can tell you whether the circuit rings, whether it's stable, and which frequencies it passes or blocks. No simulation needed. This lesson teaches the algebra behind that, and the next one shows how to read the plot.

    • get H(s) for a simple circuit by using impedances sL and 1/(sC)
    • factor H(s) and pick out its poles, zeros and gain K
    • tell the difference between the factor gain K and the DC gain H(0)

    So H(s) is just a function: put in a complex number s, get out a complex number H(s). Put in s = jω and you get the phasor gain at frequency ω. Put in other values of s and you learn more, as you'll see.

    Step 2Every RLC circuit gives a fraction of polynomials

    Each L or C brings in an s. Combine impedances in series and parallel and you always end up with one polynomial in s divided by another. This is called a rational function.

    Swap R and C in the RC low-pass, so that C is in series and R goes to ground. What is H(s)?

    Answer

    H(s) = R/(R + 1/(sC)) = sRC/(1 + sRC). There is now an s on top. This is the RC high-pass: it blocks DC, because H(0) = 0.

    K is the top's leading coefficient divided by the bottom's. For example, 2s² on top and 3s³ underneath give K = 2/3.

    Zeros (○)

    At s = z₁, a factor on top becomes zero, so H(z₁) = 0. The circuit completely blocks that "frequency". A zero on the jω axis gives a notch filter, which kills one frequency exactly.

    Poles (×)

    As s gets close to p₁, the bottom gets close to zero, so |H| shoots up towards infinity. Poles are the circuit's natural behaviour: they set how it rings and how fast it settles (remember "left decays, right grows").

    Picture |H(s)| as a stretchy rubber sheet stretched over the s-plane. Each pole is a tent pole pushing the sheet up to the sky. Each zero is a pin holding it down to the ground. The frequency response is the height of the sheet along the jω axis. Poles near that line make peaks, and zeros near it make dips.

    What if a pole and a zero sit in the same place?

    Take H(s) = (s + 1)/[(s + 1)(s + 2)]. Away from s = −1 the (s + 1) cancels, leaving 1/(s + 2). As s → −1 this tends to 1, not infinity. So −1 is not really a pole. They cancel. Always simplify before you list poles and zeros.

    Quick check

    An RC low-pass has R = 1 kΩ and C = 1 µF. Where is its pole?

    Step 4Two kinds of gain: K and H(0)

    There's a second way to write the same factors, and you'll see it on every Bode plot. Pull each root out of its bracket: s + 2 = 2(1 + s/2). In general (s − z) = (−z)(1 − s/z), as long as z ≠ 0. Then:

    Put s = 0 and every bracket becomes 1, leaving H(0). This is the DC gain: what the circuit does to a constant voltage. K and H(0) are usually different numbers. (A root at exactly zero just stays as a factor s.)

    Worked example: same function, both forms

    Multiply both out and check: each gives (4 + 2s)/(4 + 5s + s²).

    "Gain" can mean K, the DC gain H(0), or |H(jω)| at some frequency. Exam questions often mean H(0). Always check which one is being asked for.

    Quick check

    For the worked example, which pair is right?

    • Use Z_L = sL and Z_C = 1/(sC), solve the circuit, and you get H(s) as a fraction of polynomials.
    • The top's roots are zeros (H = 0 there). The bottom's roots are poles (|H| blows up there).
    • K = ratio of leading coefficients. H(0) = DC gain. They're different numbers.
