    PART 3 · SIGNALS & SYSTEMS

    What does a circuit actually do to a signal?

    What is a system?

    A function turns a number into a number. A system turns a whole signal, a voltage that changes over time, into another signal.

    A guitar pedal, the noise cancelling in your earphones, a car's suspension, the RC filter on a microcontroller pin. Each takes something that changes over time and turns it into something else that changes over time. Engineers call them all systems. Most of them can be handled with the same maths. Learn it once here and you can use it in control, communications, audio and power.

    • describe a circuit as "input signal in, output signal out"
    • explain why a capacitor gives a circuit memory, and read the time constant RC off a step response
    • say in plain words what linear and time-invariant mean, and spot a system that isn't

    Step 1Signal in, signal out

    In the AC lessons we only asked about the settled sine wave. Now we ask a bigger question. Given any input, a step, a pulse or a burst of noise, what does the whole output look like from the moment it starts?

    One important point: the output now can depend on what the input did earlier. A system can have memory.

    An ice-cream shop. The input is the list of orders coming in during the afternoon. The output is the list of ice creams handed over. If every order takes exactly two minutes, the output is just the input, two minutes late:

    The capacitor stores charge, and charge takes time to build up. That stored charge is the circuit's memory. Below, the input jumps from 0 to 1 V at t = 0 (a step), and the capacitor starts empty.

    If you double RC, does the output reach 1 V faster or slower? By how much?

    Check your guess

    Twice as slow. The output reaches 63% of its final value after one time constant, t = RC, and is within 1% after about 5RC. Double RC and every one of those times doubles.

    Grey: the input step. Teal: the capacitor voltage. A bigger RC means a slower climb.

    The time constant τ = RC is the one number that tells you how quickly an RC circuit reacts. You'll see it again as the cut-off frequency f_c = 1/(2πRC), and later as the pole at s = −1/RC. Three faces of the same number.

    Step 4Two properties that make life easy

    Linear means: double the input and the output doubles. Add two inputs and the outputs add. This is called superposition, and you've used it before in DC circuits. We always compare with the circuit starting at rest (capacitor empty).

    Time-invariant means: the circuit doesn't care what time it is. Apply the same input five seconds later and you get the same output five seconds later.

    Circuits made of ideal R, L and C with fixed values have both properties. We call them LTI. The next three lessons depend on this: if you know how an LTI circuit reacts to one very simple input, you can work out its reaction to anything.

    The precise definitions, and two systems that fail

    y(t) = x(t)² is not linear: doubling x makes y four times bigger. y(t) = t·x(t) is linear but not time-invariant: its gain depends on the clock. A diode circuit is not linear either. That's why it isn't in this course.

    Quick check

    Which statement describes a linear system?

    • A system turns an input signal into an output signal. Storage elements (C, L) give it memory.
    • An RC circuit's step response climbs with time constant τ = RC: 63% after τ, nearly done after 5τ.
    • LTI systems obey superposition and don't care about the clock. That is what makes the next lessons work.
