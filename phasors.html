    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How do we tame a sine wave?

    Capacitors and inductors become resistors.

    Write every AC signal in the right form, and the derivatives and integrals in your circuit turn into plain multiplication. Then everything you know about resistor circuits works again.

    The socket on the wall in Singapore gives 230 V at 50 Hz. The voltage swings up and down fifty times a second. If you put a capacitor across it, its current is C dv/dt. Put an inductor across it, and you need an integral. Do that for a circuit with ten parts and you are solving a pile of differential equations. This lesson shows the trick engineers use instead. By the end, a capacitor is just a "resistor" of 1/(jωC), and you can use Ohm's law, voltage dividers and nodal analysis exactly as you did for DC.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    • read the amplitude, frequency and phase from v(t) = Vₘcos(ωt + φ)
    • find the current through R, L and C when the voltage is a sinusoid, and say which one leads
    • explain why multiplying by j is the same as shifting a wave by 90°
    • use impedance (Z = R, jωL, 1/(jωC)) and phasors to solve an AC circuit like a DC one
    • find the real, reactive and complex power delivered to a load

    Step 1AC means sinusoids

    In an AC circuit the source is almost always a sinusoid. We will write every one of them as a cosine:

    Three numbers describe the whole wave:

    • Vₘ, the amplitude: the peak voltage.
    • ω, the angular frequency in rad/s. It is linked to the frequency f in hertz by ω = 2πf, and the period is T = 1/f.
    • φ, the phase: where in its cycle the wave starts at t = 0.

    A sine is the same wave a quarter cycle later, so it fits the same pattern:

    Step 2Ask each element: what current flows?

    Put the voltage v(t) = Vₘcos(ωt) across each of the three elements you meet in circuits. Before you look at the answer, try to write down the current yourself. You only need the element laws:

    Resistor

    Ohm's law. The current is the voltage, scaled down.

    Capacitor

    The current depends on how fast the voltage is changing.

    Inductor

    The current builds up as voltage is applied over time.

    For the capacitor you have to differentiate a cosine. What do you get, and is it still a cosine?

    Check your guess

    The derivative of cos(ωt) is −ω sin(ωt). That looks different, but −sin x is just cos x moved a quarter turn ahead: −sin x = cos(x + 90°). So the current is still a cosine at the same frequency. Only its size and its phase have changed.

    Left: the voltage you apply. Right: the current that flows. Purple dashed lines mark the voltage peaks and teal dashed lines the current peaks.

    An easy way to remember who leads: ELI the ICE man. In an inductor (L) the voltage E comes before the current I: E-L-I. In a capacitor (C) the current comes first: I-C-E.

    In all three cases the current is the same kind of wave as the voltage: a cosine at the same ω. The element only changes two things, the size and the phase. Hold on to that. It is the whole reason the trick in the next steps works.

    Step 4Every element now obeys Ohm's law

    For a resistor the current is proportional to the voltage: i = v/R. That is why resistor circuits are easy. Series resistors add, parallel ones combine as 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂, and nodal analysis is just algebra.

    Capacitors and inductors seemed different, because their laws have a derivative or an integral in them. Now look at what we found. Write the voltage as Re{Vejωt}, where V = Vₘejφ. Then every current has the form Re{Iejωt}, and:

    In all three, I is just V divided by something. The derivative and the integral have disappeared. So we can treat the capacitor and the inductor as "resistors" with a complex value. That value is called the impedance Z, measured in ohms:

    Left: the three element laws, each one different. Right: the same elements as impedance boxes. Every one now obeys the same rule, I = V/Z.

    And because they behave like resistors, all your DC tools come straight back:

    Voltage dividers, current dividers, KVL, KCL, nodal and mesh analysis, Thévenin and Norton all work unchanged. The only difference is that the numbers are complex.

    Check the extremes. As ω → 0 (DC), Z_C → ∞, so a capacitor acts like an open circuit, and Z_L → 0, so an inductor acts like a wire. At very high frequency it is the other way round.

    This only works when every voltage and current is a sinusoid at one frequency that has been running long enough to settle (the "steady state"). For a switch flipping on, or a square wave, you need the tools in Part 3 and Part 4.

    Step 5Save paper: the phasor

    Look at the equations in Step 4 again. Every voltage and every current has the same wrapping: a "Re{ }" on the outside and the same ejωt inside. They are the same on every line of every calculation, so they carry no information. Let's stop writing them:

    What is left, V = Vₘ∠φ, is the phasor. It is just the arrow from Step 3 frozen at t = 0. It remembers the two things that change from wire to wire: the size and the phase. ω is the same everywhere, so you note it once and set it aside.

    A phasor is an ordinary complex number, so you can write it in polar form or rectangular form, as in Lesson 1:

    The polar form (size and angle) and the rectangular form (x + jy) are the same arrow, described two ways.

    Use polar form for multiplying and dividing (multiply the sizes, add the angles). Use rectangular form for adding and subtracting.

    The recipe

    1. Convert each source to a phasor: Vₘcos(ωt + φ) → Vₘ∠φ. Turn sines into cosines first.
    2. Replace each element with its impedance: R, jωL, 1/(jωC).
    3. Solve the circuit exactly like a resistor circuit, using complex numbers.
    4. Convert back: a phasor A∠θ means A cos(ωt + θ).

    Two rules to keep in your pocket:

    • Through a capacitor: I = jωC·V. The size is multiplied by ωC and the angle goes up by 90°.
    • Through an inductor: I = V/(jωL). The size is divided by ωL and the angle goes down by 90°.

    Worked example 1: one capacitor

    v(t) = 10 cos(100t + 30°) V is applied across C = 1 mF. Find i(t).

    No derivative anywhere. The current leads the voltage by 90°, as ICE says it should.

    Worked example 2: R and C in series

    v(t) = 10 cos(100t) V drives R = 10 Ω in series with C = 1 mF. Find the current.

    Without phasors this would be a first-order differential equation. With phasors it is one division.

    Your turn. v(t) = 5 cos(50t) V is applied across L = 0.2 H. Find i(t).

    Answer

    ωL = 50 × 0.2 = 10 Ω, so Z_L = j10 Ω = 10∠90° Ω. I = 5∠0° / 10∠90° = 0.5∠−90° A, so i(t) = 0.5 cos(50t − 90°) A = 0.5 sin(50t) A. The current lags by 90°: E-L-I.

    The "230 V" on the wall is an RMS value. Its peak is 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V. The phasors on this site use peak values. Pick one convention and stick to it for the whole calculation. And only combine phasors that share the same frequency.

    Step 7Power in AC circuits

    Your electricity bill pays for energy. In a DC circuit the power is simply p = vi. In an AC circuit it is still p(t) = v(t)i(t), but now both are waves. Let's do it the long way first, then find the phasor shortcut.

    The long way

    Take v(t) = Vₘcos(ωt + θ_v) and i(t) = Iₘcos(ωt + θ_i). Multiply them, using cos A cos B = ½[cos(A − B) + cos(A + B)]:

    The second part swings up and down at twice the frequency and averages to zero over a cycle. So the average power, the part that actually does work, is the constant:

    Only the difference in phase between voltage and current matters. Slide it below.

    Right: p(t). Teal area is energy flowing into the load; orange area is energy flowing back to the source. Dashed purple: the average P. Here Vₘ = 10 V and Iₘ = 2 A.

    The phasor shortcut: complex power

    We want ½VₘIₘ and the angle difference θ_v − θ_i. Multiplying two phasors adds their angles, so first flip the sign of the current's angle. That is the complex conjugate, I* = Iₘ∠−θ_i. Then:

    • P, the real part, is the average (real) power, in watts (W). It heats, lights and turns things.
    • Q, the imaginary part, is the reactive power, in volt-amperes reactive (VAR). It is energy that sloshes back and forth between the source and the L or C each cycle. On average it does no work.
    • |S| is the apparent power, in volt-amperes (VA). The wires must be thick enough to carry it.
    • The power factor cos(θ_v − θ_i) = P/|S| tells you how much of |S| is doing useful work.

    The three form a right-angled triangle, the power triangle:

    For a single impedance Z = R + jX carrying current I, this becomes S = ½|I|²Z. The resistance takes P = ½|I|²R. The reactance gives Q = ½|I|²X: positive for an inductor, negative for a capacitor.

    Worked example

    A load has v(t) = 10 cos(100t + 30°) V and i(t) = 2 cos(100t − 15°) A.

    So P = 7.07 W, Q = 7.07 VAR and the power factor is cos 45° = 0.707. Q is positive, so the current lags: this is an inductive load, like a motor.

    Your turn. Go back to Worked example 2 in Step 5: V = 10∠0° V and I = 0.707∠45° A. Find P and Q, and check P using ½|I|²R.

    Answer

    S = ½(10∠0°)(0.707∠−45°) = 3.54∠−45° = 2.5 − j2.5 VA. So P = 2.5 W and Q = −2.5 VAR (capacitive). Check: ½ × 0.707² × 10 = ½ × 0.5 × 10 = 2.5 W. Only the resistor uses real power.

    Factories full of motors draw a lot of lagging (inductive) Q. That extra current heats the cables but does no work, so power companies charge for a poor power factor. The fix is to add capacitors, whose negative Q cancels the motors' positive Q. This is called power factor correction.

    With peak phasors, S = ½VI*. With RMS phasors (like the 230 V on the wall), the ½ disappears: S = VrmsIrms*. They give the same answer. Just don't mix them.

    Quick check

    What is the average power delivered to a pure capacitor?

    • AC sources are sinusoids, Vₘcos(ωt + φ) = Re{Vₘej(ωt+φ)}. R, L and C only change the size and phase of the current, never its frequency.
    • In exponential form, d/dt becomes ×jω, and ×j is a 90° shift. So Z_R = R, Z_L = jωL, Z_C = 1/(jωC), and V = ZI is Ohm's law for AC. Drop the Re{ } and ejωt to get the phasor Vₘ∠φ, then solve with your DC tools.
    • Complex power S = ½VI* = P + jQ. Only P does work, and P = ½VₘIₘcos(θ_v − θ_i).
