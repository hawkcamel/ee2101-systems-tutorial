LESSON 10 / SINUSOIDS & PHASORS

Sinusoids and phasors

1. Keep track of amplitude, frequency and phase

A sinusoidal voltage repeats after a period T. Its peak value is Vₘ. The phase φ tells us where in the cycle it is at t = 0. We use cosine as our reference throughout this lesson.

Frequency f is measured in hertz; angular frequency ω is in radians per second. A sine can be written as a cosine by subtracting 90° from its phase.

Teal is the fixed phasor V. Purple is V exp(jωt), which rotates as time advances. Its horizontal projection is the instantaneous voltage. Move time first; then change phase. A positive phase moves the cosine wave to the left.

2. What the phasor stores

At a known frequency, amplitude and phase are enough to specify a sinusoid. We store them in one complex number, the phasor. The common time factor is left out of the circuit calculation and restored at the end.

These phasors use peak amplitudes, not RMS values. The RMS voltage of a sinusoid is Vₘ/√2. Do not mix the two conventions in a calculation. Add phasors in rectangular form; multiply or divide them in polar form. Only add phasors directly when their frequencies are the same.

3. Replace each element by its impedance

Mark a voltage + at the terminal where the reference current enters. This is the passive sign convention. For a resistor, v(t) = Ri(t), so voltage follows current without a phase shift. Inductors and capacitors depend on a rate of change.

Why does a derivative give a 90° shift?

At a cosine peak the slope is zero. Where the cosine crosses zero on its way up, the slope is largest and positive. The derivative leads the original cosine by a quarter cycle and has amplitude ωA. Multiplying a phasor by jω does exactly this: j turns it through +90°, and ω scales its length.

Teal: current divided by its peak value. Purple: voltage divided by its peak value. Normalising lets us compare phase without putting amperes and volts on the same scale. Here R = 10 Ω, L = 0.5 H and C = 0.01 F. The formula above gives the actual peak values.

Apply the element laws

For a resistor, voltage and current are in phase. For an inductor, voltage leads current by 90°. For a capacitor, current leads voltage by 90°. Impedance is measured in ohms. Its reciprocal, admittance Y = 1/Z, is measured in siemens.

Try the circuit in the next lesson. Below its resonant frequency it behaves capacitively; above that frequency it behaves inductively.

4. Kirchhoff’s laws still apply

Voltages around a loop sum to zero and currents at a node sum to zero. Write these laws with phasors and impedances. Series impedances add. For parallel branches, add their admittances.

Application: changing phase with an RC circuit

Put a resistor in series with a capacitor and take the output across the capacitor. Its voltage ratio is 1/(1 + jωRC). Increasing frequency reduces the output amplitude and makes its phase more negative. At ωRC = 1, the gain is 1/√2 and the phase is −45°. This is a passive phase-shifting circuit; amplitude changes too.
