    PART 2 · AC CIRCUITS

    How do we tame a sine wave?

    Sine waves are spinning arrows.

    Store a sine wave's size and starting angle in one complex number, and circuits with capacitors and inductors become algebra.

    The socket on the wall in Singapore gives 230 V at 50 Hz. That voltage swings up and down fifty times every second. If you had to carry cos(100πt) through every step of a circuit calculation, with derivatives for every capacitor and inductor, you would hate this module. Phasors let you freeze the wave into one complex number. Then you solve the whole circuit the way you solved DC circuits, with Ohm's law and KVL.

    • read the amplitude, frequency and phase from v(t) = Vₘcos(ωt + φ)
    • write the phasor of a sinusoid, and turn a phasor back into a sinusoid
    • explain why an inductor or capacitor shifts the wave by a quarter cycle
    • write the impedance of R, L and C, and say what each one does at very low and very high frequency

    Step 1A sine wave needs three numbers

    Paint a dot on the rim of a spinning wheel and watch its shadow on the floor. The shadow slides back and forth in a perfect sine wave. The wheel's radius sets how far the shadow swings (amplitude). How fast the wheel spins sets the frequency. Where the dot started sets the phase.

    Vₘ is the peak voltage. f is in hertz (cycles per second). ω is the same speed measured in radians per second. φ is the starting angle. We use cosine as the reference wave throughout. A sine is the same wave a quarter cycle later:

    Set the phase to 0°, then raise it to +90°. Will the cosine wave move left or right on the time plot?

    Check your guess

    Left. A positive phase means the wave is ahead: it reaches its peak earlier. "Earlier" is to the left on a time axis. This catches a lot of people out.

    Teal is the fixed phasor V, the dot's starting position. Purple is the arrow as it spins, V ejωt. Its horizontal shadow is the voltage you would measure at that instant. Drag the time slider first and watch the purple arrow go round.

    Step 2Freeze the arrow: the phasor

    In a circuit driven by one source, every voltage and current spins at the same ω. So ω carries no information that changes from wire to wire. Each signal only needs to remember its length and its starting angle. That pair is one complex number, the phasor.

    The recipe is: drop the ejωt, do the maths with phasors, then put ejωt back and take the real part at the end.

    Write the phasor for i(t) = 2 sin(100πt) A.

    Answer

    First make it a cosine: 2 cos(100πt − 90°). The phasor is I = 2∠−90° A = −j2 A. The frequency (50 Hz) is remembered separately.

    The "230 V" on the wall is an RMS value. The peak is 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V. The phasors on this site use peak values. Pick one convention and stick to it for the whole calculation. Also, only add phasors that share the same frequency.

    Step 3Why L and C shift the wave by 90°

    A resistor is simple: v = Ri, so the voltage rises and falls exactly in step with the current. Inductors and capacitors are different. They respond to how fast something is changing, and that is what shifts the wave.

    Look at a cosine. At its peak it is flat, so its slope is zero. Where it crosses zero on the way up, it is changing fastest. So the slope wave peaks a quarter cycle before the original. In symbols:

    Taking a derivative stretches the wave by ω and moves it a quarter turn ahead. In phasor language that is just "multiply by jω". The j is the quarter turn from Lesson 1, and the ω is the stretch.

    Teal: current. Purple: voltage. Both are scaled to the same height so you can compare their timing. Here R = 10 Ω, L = 0.5 H and C = 0.01 F. Switch between the three elements and see which wave peaks first.

    Put it into the element laws

    The ratio V/I is called the impedance Z, measured in ohms. It is Ohm's law for AC.

    An easy way to remember the phase: ELI the ICE man. In an inductor (L), the voltage E comes before the current I: E-L-I. In a capacitor (C), the current comes first: I-C-E.

    Check the extremes. As ω → 0 (DC), Z_C → ∞, so a capacitor acts like an open circuit, and Z_L → 0, so an inductor acts like a wire. At very high frequency it's the other way round. With this one check you can guess what most filter circuits do before writing a single equation.

    Step 4Kirchhoff's laws still work

    Good news: nothing you learned for DC circuits is wasted. KVL (voltages round a loop add to zero) and KCL (currents into a node add to zero) work with phasors too. Impedances combine exactly like resistors:

    The only difference is that the numbers are complex.

    Example: an RC circuit that shifts the phase

    Put R in series with C and take the output across C. Voltage divider: V_out/V_in = Z_C/(R + Z_C) = 1/(1 + jωRC). At ωRC = 1 the output is 1/√2 ≈ 0.71 of the input and lags it by 45°. As the frequency goes up, the output gets smaller and lags more. This little circuit is the RC low-pass filter, and you'll meet it in almost every lesson from here on.

    • A sinusoid is a spinning arrow. Its phasor V = Vₘ∠φ is the arrow frozen at t = 0.
    • d/dt becomes ×jω, so Z_R = R, Z_L = jωL and Z_C = 1/(jωC).
    • Use KVL, KCL and series/parallel rules exactly as in DC, but with complex numbers.
